THE BRIEFING — ROME STRAIGHT UP

Rome is one of the rare famous cities that is better than its reputation, but only if you stop trying to finish it. Travelers who leave disappointed are almost always the ones who booked five sites a day and spent the week in queues; the ones who fall for it walked the same three streets twice and ate lunch slowly. Nothing here rewards efficiency. Pick two big things, book those properly, and let the rest of the days be aimless.

What makes it strange is the layering. A two-thousand-year-old temple sits in an ordinary square where people are eating lunch, and its dome is still the largest unreinforced concrete dome anywhere — with an open oculus, so rain falls into the building and drains through holes cut in the floor. The metro has only three lines because every serious excavation hits something archaeological and stops. So you walk, and the walking is the actual experience. Carry a bottle: the cast-iron street fountains Romans call nasoni run cold drinking water continuously all over the center, and refilling from them is a local habit, not a tourist trick.

It suits walkers, eaters and people who can enjoy disorder. It suits anyone who would rather sit in a piazza for an hour than tick a list. It does not suit travelers whose trip depends on things running on time, on quiet, or on air conditioning — Roman buses keep their own counsel and August apartments do not always cool. Cobbles are hard on wheeled luggage and strollers alike. And it is never empty. That is the deal you accept at the gate.

WHERE YOU'D ACTUALLY BE STANDING

TESTACCIO

South of the center, built around a covered market and the old slaughterhouse, with a hill made entirely of broken ancient amphorae at its edge. This is where Roman cooking is least softened for visitors. The tradeoff: twenty-plus minutes from the headline sights and quiet at night, with few hotels to choose from.

PRATI

Across the Tiber north of the Vatican — wide straight nineteenth-century streets, serious delicatessens, and real shops rather than souvenir stands. A calm, orderly base with Line A running through it. The tradeoff: it does not feel ancient and it does not feel especially Roman.

MONTI

The wedge between the Forum and Termini: ivy, workshops, and a small fountain square where everyone gathers with a drink in the evening. Ten minutes on foot to the Colosseum. The tradeoff: that square is loud well past midnight, and rates have long since caught up with the neighborhood's reputation.

SAN GIOVANNI

Built around the basilica that is technically the cathedral of Rome — not St Peter's, which surprises most visitors. Residential, on Line A, with proper market streets and noticeably lower prices. The tradeoff: you commute into the postcard every morning instead of waking up inside it.

WHEN TO GO — AND WHEN NOT TO

November through March is the deal window, Christmas week excepted. Days are short and there is rain, but temperatures are mild by northern standards and you can stand in the Forum without a queue behind you — which is worth more than sunshine. Late September and October bring the year's most reliable weather and the second-highest prices. April and May are lovely too, and busy with school groups.

July and August are the ones to think hard about. The heat is relentless, the stone holds it into the night, and there is very little shade among the ruins. Many family-run trattorias close for weeks around Ferragosto on August 15, when Romans leave the city entirely, so the places you were told to eat at may simply be shuttered. Holy Week and Easter spike everything across the center for papal events, and the spring marathon closes the historic streets for a day.

ONE THING TO SKIP

SKIP THE PHOTO-MENU TRATTORIAS. Any restaurant in sight of the Colosseum, the Pantheon or Piazza Navona with laminated menus in six languages, photographs of the food, and a man outside inviting you in is selling you a location, not a kitchen. You are paying a view surcharge for reheated pasta. Walk five minutes off the piazza in any direction and the price drops while the food improves, which is a very unusual bargain in a major capital. One local rule that tells you a great deal: carbonara has four ingredients and cream is not among them. If the menu is proud of its cream, keep walking.
