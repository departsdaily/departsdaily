KNOW BEFORE YOU LAND — THE SHAPE OF THE CITY

The airport is not in Detroit. DTW sits in Romulus, 20 miles southwest of downtown, and there is no train. Budget a rideshare (~$45–60) or grab the car up front — see Getting Around below for when each makes sense.
Downtown, Midtown, Corktown. Downtown is the towers, stadiums, and the People Mover loop. Midtown, up Woodward on the QLine streetcar, holds the DIA and the museums. Corktown, ten minutes west, is the 1830s Irish neighborhood where Ford spent six years restoring Michigan Central Station — a 1913 rail palace reopened in 2024 that is worth walking to just to stand in.
Skip the ruin tour. A decade ago every visitor came to photograph abandonment. The city finds it tiresome, half the famous ruins are renovated or gone, and you'd be paying to gawk at someone's block. Spend the same afternoon at Eastern Market's murals instead.

THE BRIEFING — DETROIT STRAIGHT UP

Detroit is the rare American city where the most interesting things are not downtown, and if you never leave the stadium blocks you will go home thinking you saw it. Downtown is genuinely good now — restored towers, a walkable core, crowds on game nights. It is also about six square blocks of a city that covers a hundred and forty, and the neighborhoods holding the food, the churches, the murals and the arguments are a fifteen-minute drive out in every direction.

One thing here is true nowhere else in the country: stand on the Detroit riverfront and look across the water at Canada, and you are looking south. The river bends, Windsor sits below Detroit rather than above it, and the busiest commercial border crossing in North America runs over your shoulder. It is a small geographic joke that tells you most of what you need to know about a city built around movement — of goods, of cars, of people who came for factory work from Poland, from the American South, from Yemen and Bangladesh, and stayed.

It suits people who like architecture, music history, and cities that are honest about themselves. It suits eaters especially — this is a serious food town at unserious prices. It does not suit anyone who wants to arrive without a plan and wander. Distances are long, the transit is thin outside a narrow corridor, and Detroit rewards deciding where you are going before you go. Winter is a real commitment, not a mood. Nor is this a weekend you can improvise on foot from a downtown hotel — the good version of Detroit is the one where you have picked three neighborhoods and left time to drive between them.

WHERE YOU'D ACTUALLY BE STANDING

HAMTRAMCK

An independent city of about two square miles completely surrounded by Detroit, historically Polish and now heavily Bangladeshi and Yemeni, with all three still on the same commercial strip. Bakeries next to halal butchers next to a Polish hall. There is almost nothing to book here, so treat it as a two-hour eating detour rather than a base.

NEW CENTER

Three miles up Woodward at the north end of the QLine, built as an office district when downtown ran out of room. The Fisher Building anchors it — Albert Kahn's 1928 tower with a marble and mosaic arcade running through the ground floor, free to walk. Good value on rooms, quiet at night, and you will rely on the streetcar or a car to get anywhere else.

MEXICANTOWN AND SOUTHWEST

Southwest of downtown under the approach to the Ambassador Bridge, the most continuously occupied part of the city and the densest run of taquerias and bakeries in Michigan. Come hungry, drive here, park once. Not a hotel neighborhood — it is a lunch and dinner destination that happens to be ten minutes from your bed.

WEST VILLAGE AND INDIAN VILLAGE

East of downtown along the river, blocks of enormous early-1900s houses built by auto executives, several designed by architects whose names are on downtown towers. Leafy, low-key, and increasingly where the small restaurants are opening. You will need wheels, and the surrounding blocks change character quickly.

DO IT CHEAP — THE PLAYBOOK

Pick a side in the coney war. Lafayette and American Coney Island have sold chili dogs from adjoining storefronts on Lafayette Blvd for a century. Under $6, open late, and locals will demand to know which one you chose.
Detroit-style square at the source. Buddy's on Conant Street has baked the original thick square in blue-steel automotive pans since 1946 — crispy cheese edge, sauce on top. One pizza feeds two.
Belle Isle for the price of nothing. The island state park in the Detroit River has the country's oldest aquarium (1904) and a glass conservatory. Free on foot or bike; cars need a Michigan Recreation Passport.
Free Art Deco. The Guardian Building's tiled 1929 lobby — locals call it the Cathedral of Finance — costs nothing to walk into on a weekday. Look up.
Saturday means Eastern Market. The sheds have run since 1891; the surrounding blocks now carry one of the country's densest mural collections. Go hungry, leave with produce you didn't plan on.

WHEN TO GO — AND WHEN NOT TO

January and February are the cheapest and coldest months by a wide margin — gray, lake-effect damp, genuinely below freezing for weeks. That is a fair trade if you came for museums and interiors, and a bad one if you wanted the riverfront. Late September into mid-October is the sweet spot: warm days, cold nights, and the trees along the boulevards doing their thing.

Several events reliably absorb every downtown room. The Movement electronic music festival takes Hart Plaza over Memorial Day weekend, which is fitting in the city that invented techno. The Detroit Grand Prix now runs on downtown streets in early June and closes them. Thanksgiving brings both the parade and a Lions game, and the auto show has moved to a fall slot. Check the calendar before you commit to dates — the difference between an ordinary Detroit weekend and a festival one is not subtle.

ONE THING TO SKIP

SKIP THE PEOPLE MOVER LOOP. The elevated train circling downtown is a fine three-dollar view and a terrible way to get anywhere. It is a single loop of under three miles that runs in one direction only, so half your trips take you the long way round past stations you did not want, and it does not leave the central business district at all. Ride it once for the elevated look at the Guardian and the river, then stop treating it as transit. For the Woodward corridor use the QLine streetcar; for anything else — Dearborn, Hamtramck, Belle Isle, Southwest — you want a car.
