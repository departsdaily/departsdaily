THE BRIEFING — PARIS STRAIGHT UP

Paris is a better four-day city than a ten-day one, and the people who come home underwhelmed are nearly always the ones who tried to take the monuments in order. The famous five are scattered across the city, each one costs you a queue and a Metro transfer, and doing all of them in seventy-two hours means the trip becomes a logistics exercise with croissants. Two of them, plus one neighbourhood you walked until your feet hurt, is a better holiday and everyone who has been more than twice knows it.

The city is easier to read than it looks once you learn one thing: the twenty arrondissements spiral clockwise outward from the 1st, which sits at the Louvre, to the 20th out east — locals call the shape the escargot. The postcode does the rest of the work. 75004 is the Marais, 75011 is east of Bastille, 75018 is Montmartre. Read the last two digits of any hotel address and you know within a few streets where you will be standing, which no other capital hands you for free.

It suits walkers above everyone else — the city inside the ring road is only about six miles across and Metro stations sit a few hundred metres apart, so distances that look daunting rarely are. It does not suit travellers who need everything open on demand. The Louvre closes on Tuesdays and the Musée d'Orsay on Mondays, plenty of smaller places shut over lunch, and in August a lot of family-run bakeries and bistros close for weeks while their owners are at the coast. Several national museums are free on the first Sunday of the month, which is a gift or a queue depending entirely on your patience.

WHERE YOU'D ACTUALLY BE STANDING

THE 11TH — OBERKAMPF TO BASTILLE

East of the Marais, residential and young, with more small restaurants per block than anywhere truly central and prices that have not been set for visitors. Fits people who came to eat and stay out. The tradeoff is that there is nothing famous to look at here, and the bar streets are loud until late on weekends.

CANAL SAINT-MARTIN — 10TH

Iron footbridges, plane trees and working locks, with the canal bank serving as the city's living room on warm evenings. Fits travellers who would rather live somewhere for a week than sightsee from it. The tradeoff is a longer walk to the Seine and a stretch near the northern stations that feels less comfortable after dark.

MONTMARTRE — 18TH

A hill village the city grew around: staircases instead of streets, small squares, and the white basilica at the top. Fits early risers who will be out before the coaches arrive. The tradeoff is physical — the climbs are steep, the Metro is at the bottom of them, and the summit is shoulder-to-shoulder by mid-morning.

BATIGNOLLES — 17TH

Quiet blocks around a small English-style park north of Saint-Lazare, more neighbourhood than destination, with a village market feel on weekends. Fits families and anyone who has had enough of crowds. The tradeoff is straightforward: you commute in to the sights every single day.

WHEN TO GO — AND WHEN NOT TO

January through mid-March and again in November are the cheap months. It is grey, often wet, and dark by five — but flights and hotels both drop, restaurants have tables, and you can stand in front of a famous painting with nobody's elbow in your ribs. Late August is the underrated window: rates ease and the city empties out because Parisians take the month off. The catch is that a share of the small bakeries and bistros are shut for exactly the same reason.

Peak is June through early September plus the fortnight around Christmas. Fashion weeks in late February or early March and again in late September or early October spike hotel rates without appearing on most travellers' radar, and the late-May-into-June tennis fortnight does the same. Bastille Day on July 14 fills everything. One practical warning: July and August heat waves have become routine and many older Paris hotels have no air conditioning — check before you book a top-floor room. Late September into October trades weather against price better than any other window.

ONE THING TO SKIP

SKIP THE MONA LISA SCRUM. In the Salle des États you shuffle through a switchback barrier, get perhaps thirty seconds at a distance from a small painting behind glass with several hundred phones raised in front of you, and are moved along. Meanwhile the Italian galleries either side of it — the Denon wing, enormous canvases, some of the strangest things in the building — are often close to empty, because everyone is standing in that one queue. Walk past it. Book a timed entry, choose two wings, look properly at fifteen things, and leave before your attention runs out. The greatest-hits route is how people end up saying museums are exhausting.
