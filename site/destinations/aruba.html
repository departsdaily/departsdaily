THE BRIEFING — ARUBA STRAIGHT UP

Aruba is a desert, and the fastest way to be disappointed here is to book a rainforest in your head. The interior is cactus, aloe fields, boulder outcrops and low thorn scrub; the island takes less than two feet of rain in a normal year, and most of that falls in short bursts between October and January. Nothing is green in the way Jamaica or Costa Rica is green. What Aruba has instead is a west coast of wide, shallow, unusually calm water and a trade wind that never stops blowing, which is why 88 degrees here does not feel like 88 degrees in Miami.

It is small — roughly twenty miles end to end — and remarkably easy for an American traveler. English is spoken everywhere alongside Dutch and Papiamento, the local creole you will hear only on Aruba, Bonaire and Curacao, and US dollars are accepted as a matter of routine. The detail that matters most for the flight home: the airport has US preclearance, so you clear American immigration and customs before you board and land in the States as a domestic arrival. That is convenient at the far end and brutal at this one. Give yourself far more airport time than the island feels like it needs.

It suits couples, first-time Caribbean travelers, cruise passengers who want a second look, and anyone who values a beach that behaves — the leeward water is flat and clear most days of the year. It does not suit hikers looking for altitude, or anyone whose idea of an island is jungle and waterfalls. The highest point is a hill you can climb in under half an hour.

WHERE YOU'D ACTUALLY BE STANDING

PALM BEACH

The high-rise strip in the Noord district, and the busiest sand on the island — casinos, chain kitchens, water sports desks, and a daily dawn ritual of towels claiming palapas. It fits people who want everything reachable on foot after dark. The tradeoff is that you are never alone on that beach.

EAGLE BEACH

Just south, and a different island entirely: low-rise buildings, a beach wide enough to lose your group on, and the wind-bent divi-divi trees that point permanently southwest. It fits couples and readers. The tradeoff is that most hotels sit across the road rather than on the sand, and there is less to walk to at night.

ORANJESTAD

The capital and cruise port, with Dutch colonial facades painted in sherbet colors and a free streetcar running the main street. Staying here gets you a town rather than a resort strip, and generally lower rates. The tradeoff is obvious — no swimming beach at your door, and it goes quiet the moment the ships leave.

SAN NICOLAS

The old refinery town at the southern end, now covered in large-scale murals from an annual art festival, with the shallow protected lagoon of Baby Beach nearby. Worth a day even if you sleep elsewhere. The tradeoff is distance: it is roughly forty minutes from the hotel strips and very quiet after dark.

WHEN TO GO — AND WHEN NOT TO

Mid-April through early December is when Aruba is cheap, and the weather is essentially the same as it is in February — the island sits south of the main hurricane track, so direct hits are rare and the low season is a pricing convention rather than a weather event. Temperatures barely move all year. The wettest stretch is October to January, and even then it tends to be a shower rather than a day. If your only constraint is money, book September or October and stop reading.

The expensive window is mid-December through mid-April, with Christmas and New Year the single most costly week of the year and worth avoiding unless someone else is paying. Carnival is the other one: the season runs from early January to the grand parade the Sunday before Ash Wednesday, and the weeks around it tighten both flights and rooms. Trade winds blow hardest from roughly February into June — excellent for windsurfers, irritating for beach umbrellas.

ONE THING TO SKIP

SKIP SWIMMING THE NORTH COAST. The windward side of the island — the stretch running past Andicuri, Boca Prins, Dos Playa and around to Boca Grandi — is spectacular to look at and genuinely dangerous to enter. There is real Atlantic swell, a strong pull off the sand, sharp ironshore rock in places, and no lifeguards anywhere on that coast. People drown there, and they are usually visitors who saw an empty beautiful beach and assumed empty meant safe. Photograph it, walk it, drive it, then do your swimming on the leeward west coast or in the shallow protected lagoon at the southern tip. The only sanctioned dip on that side is the natural pool, and even that depends on the day.
