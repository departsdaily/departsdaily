THE BRIEFING — PHILADELPHIA STRAIGHT UP

Philadelphia gives you more city per dollar than anywhere else on the Northeast Corridor, and it is faintly insulted that you needed convincing. It was the capital until 1800, lost the job to a swamp on the Potomac, and has spent two centuries being comfortable about it instead of anxious. The upshot for a traveler is a city with a UNESCO-listed hall, a serious art museum, a covered market running since the 1890s, and hotel rates that look like a rounding error next to the same weekend in New York — ninety minutes up the same rail line.

The layout is the thing people underrate. William Penn laid out a grid in 1682 with five public squares and City Hall in the middle, numbered streets running one way and tree names — Chestnut, Walnut, Spruce, Pine — running the other. Learn that once and you never need a map again. By gentlemen's agreement nothing rose above the brim of Penn's hat on top of City Hall until 1987, which is why the skyline has an obvious before and after. Center City is walkable end to end in about half an hour, and most of what you flew in for sits inside it.

It suits walkers, eaters, museum people, and anyone who wants a two-night city break without a four-figure hotel bill. It suits sports fans unreasonably well — all four pro teams play in one South Philly complex you can reach on a single subway line. It does not suit anyone shopping for polish. Philadelphia is blunt, patched, occasionally grubby, and will not soften any of that for a visitor. If you need a city to flatter you, go somewhere else.

WHERE YOU'D ACTUALLY BE STANDING

FISHTOWN

Old rowhouse blocks northeast of Center City that turned into the city's music-and-bar district along Frankford Avenue. Right for people who want to be out late among locals rather than tourists — the Market-Frankford El gets you downtown in minutes. The tradeoff: almost no hotels, so you are in a rental, and weekend nights are loud.

EAST PASSYUNK

South Philly, where Passyunk Avenue cuts diagonally across Penn's grid and creates a string of odd triangular corners full of restaurants. Residential, quiet by eleven, and where you eat if food is why you came. The tradeoff: fifteen minutes on the Broad Street Line from the historic core, and again, thin on rooms.

WASHINGTON SQUARE WEST

The blocks between Broad Street and Independence Mall, including the Gayborhood with its rainbow-striped street signs. You can walk to the founding sites, the market and the theaters without ever hailing anything. The tradeoff: it is busy after dark and fairly plain by daylight — convenience, not charm.

UNIVERSITY CITY

Across the Schuylkill, built around Penn and Drexel, with 30th Street Station on the doorstep for Amtrak and regional rail. Cheaper beds and student-priced food. The tradeoff: it reads as a campus more than a neighborhood, empties out in summer, and you cross the river for everything else.

WHEN TO GO — AND WHEN NOT TO

January and February are the bargain and they are genuinely grim — gray, damp, wind funneling down Broad Street. You trade weather for empty museums and the year's lowest room rates, which for a two-day history trip is a fair deal. Late August is the other soft spot: hot, thickly humid, and the city has a smell in August that regulars simply accept. Spring and fall are the pleasant months and the expensive ones.

Specific spikes to plan around: the flower show in early March fills Center City hotels solid, the July 4 week of free events on the Parkway prices like a holiday because it is one, and September through October is convention season at the riverfront center. The Mummers Parade up Broad Street on New Year's Day is the one January date the city is full. Home-game weekends in South Philly tighten rooms too.

ONE THING TO SKIP

SKIP THE 2AM CHEESESTEAK CORNER. The intersection in South Philly where two neon-lit rivals face each other is a tourist ritual, not a meal. You will queue behind a bachelor party, order under pressure in a dialect you do not speak, and eat a mediocre sandwich standing up at two in the morning. Nobody who lives here does this. Every neighborhood has a corner steak shop that does it better for less, and the sandwich Philadelphians actually argue about is roast pork with sharp provolone and broccoli rabe. Order that instead, at a place with no bus parked outside, and you will understand what the fuss was supposed to be.
