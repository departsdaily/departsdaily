THE BRIEFING — PHOENIX STRAIGHT UP

Phoenix is a valley of twenty-odd cities pretending to be one, and if you fly in looking for a downtown you will spend the week disappointed and in traffic. What it actually sells is winter sunshine, mountains inside the city limits, and a resort culture built entirely around a pool — and at that it is very good. Judge it as a basecamp, not a city, and it delivers. Judge it as a walkable urban break and it fails on day one.

The geography is simple once someone explains it. The Valley of the Sun sits in a bowl ringed by ranges: South Mountain below, Camelback and Piestewa in the middle of everything, the McDowells northeast, the Superstitions out east. Central Avenue splits the metro — numbered Avenues run west of it, numbered Streets run east — and that one rule orients you across most of the Valley. The saguaro forests you came for grow only in the Sonoran Desert and nowhere else on earth, which is why the skyline of cactus arms looks unlike any other American landscape.

It suits pool-and-trailhead travelers, golfers, spring-training fans, families escaping a real winter, and anyone happy to drive. It does not suit people without a car — the light rail is useful for a corridor and useless for everything else. It does not suit anyone who wants to walk between dinner and a bar without checking a map. One planning quirk: Arizona ignores daylight saving, so in summer the state runs on Pacific time and in winter on Mountain, and your flight connections will look wrong until you remember that.

WHERE YOU'D ACTUALLY BE STANDING

OLD TOWN SCOTTSDALE

The one genuinely walkable pocket in the Valley — galleries, patios and bars inside a few blocks, with the canal path running along the edge. Right if you want to park the car once and leave it. The tradeoff: highest rates in the metro, half an hour from the airport, and the bar blocks get very loud on weekend nights.

TEMPE

University territory around Mill Avenue and the man-made town lake, and the easiest place to reach without a car — the airport Sky Train links to light rail that runs straight through it. The cheapest beds in the Valley tend to sit here. The tradeoff: undergraduate volume during term, and a strange emptiness in the summer months.

ARCADIA AND THE CAMELBACK CORRIDOR

Ranch houses, old citrus groves and mid-century blocks tucked under Camelback Mountain, dead center of the metro and closest to the good trailheads. Made for hikers who want to be at the trailhead by sunrise. The tradeoff: it is residential, so you need a car and there is little at the budget end.

DOWNTOWN PHOENIX

Arenas, the ballpark, museums and the light rail spine, plus the mural blocks just north. Good value when an event is on and you want to walk home from it. The tradeoff: on a quiet Tuesday it can feel deserted after dark, and the pavement radiates heat well into the night.

WHEN TO GO — AND WHEN NOT TO

June through August is when the fares and the resort rates collapse, because it is 105 to 115 degrees and everyone sensible has left. That trade is real and it works if you accept the schedule: outside at dawn, pool at dusk, indoors in between. The monsoon runs from July into September and brings humidity, dust storms that arrive as a brown wall, and evening thunder. November through April is the payoff season — dry, clear, high seventies — and it is priced exactly like the reward it is.

The spikes are worth mapping. All of March is Cactus League spring training, with fifteen major league clubs camped across the Valley and hotel rates to match. The early-February golf tournament in Scottsdale draws crowds bigger than most stadiums hold. The January collector-car auctions fill Scottsdale for a week. Add Christmas through New Year and spring break, and the entire good-weather calendar is essentially one long high season.

ONE THING TO SKIP

SKIP MIDDAY SUMMER SUMMIT HIKES. Camelback's two routes are not walks, they are steep scrambles with handrails, no shade, no water on the mountain and no easy way down once you are cooked. The city closes some trails outright during excessive heat warnings, and crews still fly rescues off that mountain every summer for visitors who started at ten in the morning with one bottle of water. If you are here between May and September, you hike at first light or you do not hike. There is no version of this where you push through it. The desert does not negotiate and the view is identical at 5:30am.
