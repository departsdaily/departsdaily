THE BRIEFING — AUSTIN STRAIGHT UP

Austin has not been a cheap college town for at least a decade, and any guide still selling it that way is working from old notes. The skyline downtown is almost entirely newer than the people describing it, the hotel market behaves like a coastal city whenever an event is in town, and the slacker-paradise story is now largely nostalgia. What survives from that Austin is the part that actually mattered: live music somewhere every night of the week, and water you can swim in inside the city limits.

The geography explains more than the culture does. Austin sits directly on the Balcones Escarpment, the fault line where the Texas Hill Country drops away to the coastal plain. West of it you get limestone hills, clear creeks and cold springs; east of it you get flat blackland prairie. That is why a city in Central Texas has spring-fed swimming holes at all, and also why sudden hard rain here runs off rock and fills creeks fast. Downtown, the Texas Capitol stands taller than the United States Capitol in Washington, which is either a fun fact or the entire state explained in one sentence.

It suits people who intend to be outside and out late, who will happily eat standing up, and who do not need a plan past lunch. It does not suit anyone who wilts in heat — summer runs long and triple digits arrive in streaks — or anyone expecting a compact walkable core. The good parts sit in pockets connected by short rides, and transit between them is thin. Allergy sufferers should also know about cedar fever: mountain cedar pollen peaks from December into February and flattens people every year.

WHERE YOU'D ACTUALLY BE STANDING

RAINEY STREET DISTRICT

A short strip of century-old bungalows converted into bars, now boxed in on all sides by new glass towers, at the southeast corner of downtown near the lake. It fits people who want to walk home from the last drink. The tradeoff is noise until closing time and a scene that skews young and loud.

SOUTH CONGRESS

The shopping and eating strip running south from the river, with the downtown skyline framed dead center as you look back up the hill. It fits first-timers who want to stroll rather than plan. The tradeoff is that everyone knows it — sidewalks jam on weekends, and prices reflect the address.

EAST AUSTIN

Across Interstate 35 from downtown, historically Mexican-American and Black neighborhoods now layered with bars, taquerias and galleries. It fits repeat visitors who want the current version of the city. The tradeoff is the interstate itself: crossing it on foot is grim, so budget for rides.

ZILKER AND BARTON HILLS

Residential blocks south of the river beside the park and the spring-fed pool, backing onto the greenbelt trail. It fits anyone whose plan is swimming and walking. The tradeoff is that there are almost no hotels — mostly short-term rentals — and the park becomes a fenced festival ground for two weekends each October.

WHEN TO GO — AND WHEN NOT TO

The cheap windows are January and February, and then again in the deep heat of late July and August, when triple-digit days run in streaks and rates drop to compensate. August is a genuine bargain if you accept that you will be indoors or in water between noon and six. March through May and the back half of October are the pleasant months, and they are priced exactly like the pleasant months.

Then there are the weeks where a cheap fare means nothing because there is no room to sleep in. South by Southwest takes mid-March and multiplies every rate in the city. Austin City Limits fills two weekends in early October. The Formula 1 race at the circuit southeast of town takes an autumn weekend and drags rates up across the whole metro. University home football Saturdays from September into November do the same on a smaller scale, and May graduation weekends are worse than people expect. Check the calendar before you book the flight, not after.

ONE THING TO SKIP

SKIP DIRTY SIXTH AFTER DARK. The stretch of East Sixth Street between the interstate and Congress closes to traffic on weekend nights and turns into a drink-special corridor built for twenty-one-year-olds. The bands are playing covers, the sidewalks are sticky, the crowd is enormous, and it is the single worst place in Austin to hear the music the city is famous for. It is also where visitors most reliably have a bad night. Walk it once for ten minutes so you have seen it, then go north to the Red River venues, east across the interstate, or south to Congress Avenue — all three are where the actual players are working while you are queuing for a plastic cup.
