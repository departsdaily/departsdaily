THE BRIEFING — WASHINGTON DC STRAIGHT UP

The National Mall is not a park, and treating it like one is the classic Washington mistake. It is two miles of open, shadeless ground running from the Capitol to the Lincoln Memorial, with the museums strung along its edges — so a casual plan to do the Mall today turns into six or seven miles on gravel with the sun directly overhead and nowhere to sit. Pick two museums, use the Metro to jump between the far ends, and you will enjoy the same city as the people wilting on benches at two in the afternoon.

Washington has no skyscrapers, and that is a matter of federal law rather than taste: a 1910 act ties how tall a building can be to the width of the street in front of it, which caps the skyline around a dozen storeys and leaves the Capitol dome and the Washington Monument still visibly in charge. The other local rule worth learning before you land is the quadrant system. Addresses are numbered outward from the Capitol into NW, NE, SW and SE, and the same street number exists in all four — an address given without its two letters can be four different places, several miles apart.

It suits anyone travelling on a budget with an interest in history, because the expensive part of most city trips — admission — is simply free here, and it suits first-time solo travellers because the Metro is clean, legible and goes where you need. It does not suit nightlife-first travellers; the city eats early and empties on Sunday. And it does not suit anyone allergic to crowds who insists on visiting in April.

WHERE YOU'D ACTUALLY BE STANDING

DUPONT CIRCLE

Embassy townhouses and bookshops around a fountain circle, with a Red Line station in the middle of it and a Sunday farmers market. Walk or ride one short hop to the Mall. Fits adults who want dinner and a bar within a block of the bed. Tradeoff: it is the most expensive of the residential options and rooms run small.

U STREET AND SHAW

The historic spine of Black Washington, where the theatres and jazz rooms of Duke Ellington's city still stand and still operate, now surrounded by the strongest restaurant corridor in town. Green and Yellow lines run underneath. Fits night people. Tradeoff: weekend noise, and a longer haul to the monuments.

CAPITOL HILL AND EASTERN MARKET

Rowhouses in ice-cream colours behind the Capitol, built around an 1873 brick market hall with a flea market beside it at weekends. Quiet, safe, walkable to the Mall's eastern end and the Library of Congress. Tradeoff: restaurants close early and it is a genuinely residential neighbourhood, not an entertainment one.

ROSSLYN AND CRYSTAL CITY

Across the Potomac in Arlington, one or two Metro stops from the Mall and minutes from DCA. Rooms are typically cheaper and larger, and the view back at the monuments as you cross the bridge is worth the compromise on its own. Tradeoff: office-district evenings — you commute to your own holiday.

WHEN TO GO — AND WHEN NOT TO

January and February are the cheap months and the secret good ones. It is cold and grey, Congress is often out, and the museums that are shoulder to shoulder in April are nearly empty — you can stand in front of a famous object alone. August is the other soft window: swampy, heavy, thunderstormy, with much of official Washington on recess and the city noticeably emptier. Late September through October brings the most comfortable weather of the year with prices sitting in the middle.

The spike to plan around is cherry blossom season. Peak bloom at the Tidal Basin usually falls in the last week of March or the first week of April, the Park Service only forecasts the dates a couple of weeks out, and hotels price the whole surrounding month as if it will hit. School spring break stacks on top of it. May brings graduation weekends, July 4 fills the Mall for fireworks, and every fourth January an inauguration takes the entire city off the market.

ONE THING TO SKIP

DON'T BOOK A GEORGETOWN HOTEL. Georgetown is the prettiest neighbourhood in Washington and the only significant one with no Metro station — the subway was never built out there. So every trip you take begins with a fifteen to twenty minute uphill walk to Foggy Bottom or Dupont, or a bus, and it ends the same way at midnight. You will pay a premium for cobblestones and then spend your week getting away from them. Go for an afternoon instead: the canal towpath, the waterfront, the steps from the film, and dinner. Then sleep somewhere with a train under it, which in this city is the only amenity that actually matters.
