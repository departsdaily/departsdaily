THE BRIEFING — NEW YORK CITY STRAIGHT UP

New York punishes the itinerary. Pick two neighbourhoods and one thing you paid for, and you will come home liking the city more than the person who tried to see all of it in three days. That is the whole trick. Charlotte to New York is a two-hour flight, which makes it a weekend rather than a holiday, and weekend visitors chronically overbook — four boroughs, six landmarks, an hour on a train between each, and no memory of anything except being tired on a platform.

The city is far easier to read than its reputation suggests. Above 14th Street Manhattan follows the 1811 grid — numbered streets running east to west, avenues running north to south, every address telling you roughly where it is. Broadway is the one exception, an older route that cuts diagonally across the whole thing, and everywhere it crosses an avenue it opens a square: Union, Madison, Herald, Times. That single fact is why those squares exist at all, and once you see it Manhattan stops being confusing and starts being obvious.

It suits people who like walking and do not mind noise, and it rewards anyone willing to eat where the queue is short. It does not suit anyone who wants quiet or expects to drive. Which airport your cheap fare uses genuinely changes the day: LaGuardia is closest but has no rail link — the planned AirTrain was cancelled in 2022 — so it is a bus to the Jackson Heights subway hub or a car. JFK connects by AirTrain to the subway or the LIRR at Jamaica, and Newark by AirTrain to New Jersey Transit into Penn Station. Check that before you judge the fare.

WHERE YOU'D ACTUALLY BE STANDING

LONG ISLAND CITY

The Queens waterfront one stop from Midtown on the 7 or the E, where a cluster of newer hotels sits well under anything comparable across the river. Gantry Plaza State Park puts the Manhattan skyline directly in front of you at sunset. The tradeoff is that it is largely a tower-and-office district, and dinner options thin out fast once you leave the main avenue.

EAST VILLAGE

Walk-ups, tenement blocks and Tompkins Square Park, with the densest run of small bars and cheap restaurants left in Manhattan. Fits people who intend to be out late and eat at eleven. The tradeoff is transit — only a few lines reach it, so you walk to trains — and weekend nights are loud through the window.

UPPER WEST SIDE

Central Park on one side, Riverside Park on the other, pre-war apartment buildings in between, with several subway lines running the length of it. Fits families and anyone who wants the day to end at a reasonable hour. The tradeoff is roughly twenty minutes to downtown and an evening that closes early by New York standards.

WILLIAMSBURG

First stop in Brooklyn on the L train, or a ferry ride across the East River. Low-rise blocks, waterfront parks, and the skyline looking back at you. Fits a second visit, when the landmarks are done. The tradeoff is dependence on one subway line — weekend track work turns a ten-minute hop into an ordeal.

WHEN TO GO — AND WHEN NOT TO

The cheapest stretch is late January through February. It is cold, the wind funnels down the avenues, and it is dark by five — but hotel rates fall hard, restaurants take walk-ins, and the museums are practically yours. The other dip is early to mid August, humid and sticky with a lot of locals out of town. If you want the weather instead of the discount, late April into early June and late September into October are the two genuinely pleasant windows.

Expensive is Thanksgiving through New Year's, when the tree, the markets and New Year's Eve push December hotel rates to the year's peak. September is the sleeper problem: the UN General Assembly closes Midtown streets and fills the East Side, and fashion weeks in February and September add their own spike. The marathon takes the first Sunday in November and the US Open runs late August into early September, which fills Queens and everything on the 7 line.

ONE THING TO SKIP

SKIP THE SECOND OBSERVATION DECK. You will be sold four or five of them and they are all looking at the same island from roughly the same height. Pick one, book a timed slot near sunset, and stop. There is a decent argument that a skyline view is improved by having the Empire State Building in it, which is precisely the view you cannot get from standing on it. Put the second deck's money into something with a different vantage — the Brooklyn Bridge walkway early in the morning, a ferry across the harbour, or a hotel bar where the height costs the price of one drink. And skip the multi-attraction passes unless you will genuinely do four things.
