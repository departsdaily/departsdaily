THE BRIEFING — HOUSTON STRAIGHT UP

Houston is the least attractive great food city in America and it does not care what you think about that. There is no skyline moment, no historic quarter, no walk that sells the place. What there is instead is the fourth-largest city in the country, the most ethnically diverse one by most measures, and a restaurant scene that runs from Gulf Coast Vietnamese to central Texas barbecue to Nigerian and Salvadoran cooking, mostly in strip malls, mostly for very little money.

The reason it looks the way it does is genuinely unusual: Houston is the largest city in the United States with no formal zoning code. Voters have rejected zoning at the ballot box more than once. The result is a townhouse next to a machine shop next to a taqueria next to a glass tower, endlessly, in every direction — a city that grew by deed restriction and market logic rather than by plan. Once you know that, the sprawl stops reading as ugly and starts reading as legible.

It suits eaters, space nerds, and anyone who likes museums — the Museum District packs nineteen institutions into a walkable cluster, and several of them are permanently free, including the Menil Collection and the Rothko Chapel in Montrose. It does not suit people without a car, and it does not suit anyone who visits in the wrong month. From June through September the humidity is the whole story, and the smart version of a Houston summer trip happens indoors. Everything here is a twenty-five-minute drive from everything else; accept that on arrival and the city opens up.

WHERE YOU'D ACTUALLY BE STANDING

MONTROSE

The one part of Houston that behaves like an old neighborhood — bungalows, live oaks, the city's oldest gay district, and the Menil campus sitting in the middle of it like a park. Best base if you want to walk to dinner. Rooms here are mostly rentals and small properties rather than hotels, and parking is street-side.

THE HEIGHTS

Northwest of downtown on a low rise that keeps it out of the worst flooding, with Victorian houses, a bike trail on an old rail bed, and antique shops turning into restaurants. It is quieter and more residential than Montrose and better for a longer stay. Nothing else is walkable from it, so plan on driving in.

EADO (EAST DOWNTOWN)

The blocks east of the freeway, formerly warehouses, now soccer and baseball crowds, breweries, and a long run of murals. Cheap by downtown standards and on the light rail line, which puts the Museum District a few stops south. It empties out fast on non-game nights and the blocks between the good corners are still parking lots.

RICE VILLAGE AND THE MUSEUM DISTRICT

South of downtown around the university and the museums, leafy and low-slung with Hermann Park in the middle. This is the easiest area to spend three days without your car, and the closest to the medical center if that is why you are here. It is also the most conventional corner of the city.

WHEN TO GO — AND WHEN NOT TO

October and November are the reward months — warm, dry-ish, and the only stretch where being outside all day is pleasant. Late January and February are the cheapest and are genuinely mild, with the occasional hard freeze that the city handles badly. July and August are cheap because they are punishing; if you go then, plan around the museums, the tunnels, and an early morning.

The two spikes to know are local and large. The Houston Livestock Show and Rodeo runs about three weeks from late February into March and is one of the biggest events of its kind anywhere — hotel rates across the city move for it. In early May the offshore energy conference brings tens of thousands of engineers to town and does the same thing, more sharply and with less warning to anyone who does not work in oil. Hurricane season peaks from August into September.

ONE THING TO SKIP

SKIP DOWNTOWN ON A WEEKEND. Booking downtown because it is central is the standard first-timer mistake here. Houston's downtown is an office district, and the celebrated tunnel system beneath it — miles of climate-controlled passages full of lunch counters — is open on weekday business hours and shut when you are most likely to be exploring. On a Saturday you get quiet streets, closed lobbies, and a rideshare to every meal. Unless you are in town for a convention or a game, stay in Montrose, the Heights, or near the Museum District, where the kitchens are open on the days you are actually free and the walk to them is short.
