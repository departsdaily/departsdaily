THE BRIEFING — PUNTA CANA STRAIGHT UP

Punta Cana is not a city break and anyone selling it as one is misleading you. It is roughly thirty miles of gated resort compounds strung along a genuinely excellent beach, and the resort you pick is the trip — not a base for the trip, the trip itself. Once you accept that, it becomes one of the easiest and most forgiving weeks in the Caribbean. If you were hoping to wander into town for dinner and stumble on somewhere local, you have booked the wrong island coast.

The strip runs north to south: Uvero Alto at the top, then Macao, Arena Gorda, Bávaro, Cabeza de Toro, Punta Cana proper and Cap Cana at the bottom. A reef sits offshore along much of the Bávaro stretch, which is why the water there is shallow, bathwater-warm and flat, while Macao a few miles north gets real surf. The whole coast is planted with coconut palms, and the airport itself is open-air under palm-thatched roofs — you walk off the plane into the weather rather than into a jet bridge, which tells you what kind of place this intends to be.

It suits people who want zero decisions for a week: families, groups, milestone birthdays, first-timers to the Caribbean, anyone who wants the bill settled before departure. It does not suit independent eaters and walkers. There is no old town, restaurants outside the gates require a taxi both ways, and the country's real historic city is hours away by road. Be honest with yourself about resort tier, too — the cheap end is perfectly fine on beach and pool, but food quality is where the price difference actually shows up.

WHERE YOU'D ACTUALLY BE STANDING

BAVARO AND ARENA GORDA

The main event: resorts wall to wall on reef-calmed water, excursion desks and vendors working the sand, and miles of beach you can actually walk. Suits first-timers and families who want the classic version. The tradeoff: it is busy and makes no apology for it — you will never have that sand to yourself.

CAP CANA

A gated development south of the airport built around a marina, with a calm turquoise cove at Juanillo. Manicured, quiet, and leans adults and honeymooners. The tradeoff: it is expensive and a little sterile, and there is genuinely nothing to walk to beyond the gates.

UVERO ALTO

Forty-five minutes to an hour north of the airport, newer and larger resorts on a wilder, surfier stretch of coast. Right if you want the beach to feel close to empty. The tradeoff: a long transfer at both ends, and once you are there, leaving for anything is a half-day project.

CABEZA DE TORO

A quieter pocket between Bávaro and the airport with fewer properties, calm reef-sheltered water and the shortest ride from your flight. Suits travelers who want Bávaro's sea without Bávaro's crowd. The tradeoff: a short strip means fewer resorts to choose from and less nearby.

WHEN TO GO — AND WHEN NOT TO

The cheap windows are May into June and September into November, and there is a reason for the second one. Atlantic hurricane season runs June through November with the risk concentrated from mid-August into October — exactly when rates fall hardest. That window is bookable, but book it with refundable fares and real travel insurance rather than optimism. Sargassum seaweed is the other seasonal variable: it drifts onto east-facing Caribbean beaches mainly from spring into summer, varies enormously year to year, and resorts rake it daily, but a bad week is a real possibility.

High season is mid-December through April — dry, breezy with the trade winds, and the most expensive rooms of the year. Christmas through New Year and Holy Week around Easter are the absolute peaks and want booking months ahead. March brings North American spring break, which changes the crowd at many resorts noticeably. If you want the good weather without the peak pricing, late April and early May is the sweet spot.

ONE THING TO SKIP

SKIP THE SANTO DOMINGO DAY TRIP. It is roughly three hours each way by highway. That means six-plus hours on a bus to spend maybe two hours walking the Colonial Zone before turning around, and you will be exhausted for a day and a half afterward. The Colonial Zone genuinely deserves your time — it is the oldest continuously inhabited European-founded settlement in the Americas, with a cathedral to match — which is exactly why it should not be a coach excursion squeezed between two beach days. Give it two nights at the start or end of the trip, or leave it for a different holiday entirely. Do not do it in a day.
