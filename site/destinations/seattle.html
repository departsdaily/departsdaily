THE BRIEFING — SEATTLE STRAIGHT UP

Seattle's rain reputation is mostly a slander with a technicality inside it: the city gets less precipitation in a year than New York, Atlanta or Miami. What it gets instead is around 150 grey, drizzly, low-ceilinged days spread from October to May — annoying, but rarely the kind of weather that cancels a plan. Locals do not carry umbrellas, they carry hoods. For a traveller flying in on a cheap winter fare, that distinction is the whole argument.

The thing here that is not anywhere else: at the Ballard Locks the city runs a working ship canal between salt water and fresh, and built a fish ladder beside it with underwater viewing windows. In summer you stand at glass and watch sockeye and coho climb rung by rung while a fishing boat is lifted twenty feet in the chamber next door. It costs nothing, it is a genuine piece of civil engineering rather than an attraction, and it is twenty minutes from downtown. Pike Place, for its part, has operated continuously since 1907.

Seattle suits people who want a city with an exit — mountains, water and ferries all reachable inside an hour — and who like coffee, seafood and bookshops more than nightlife. It does not suit anyone who needs guaranteed sun, and it does not suit anyone expecting a compact, flat walking city. The hills are steep enough to be a decision, the good neighbourhoods are separated by water and drawbridges, and getting between them takes longer than the map suggests.

WHERE YOU'D ACTUALLY BE STANDING

CAPITOL HILL

Up the hill east of downtown, the densest run of bars, restaurants and record shops in the city, wrapped around Cal Anderson Park. Light rail puts you downtown in a few minutes. Fits people who plan to be out after ten. The tradeoff is weekend street noise and the fact that every walk home is uphill.

BALLARD

The old Scandinavian fishing neighbourhood by the locks — low brick blocks, a farmers market that runs every Sunday of the year, boatyards still working. The calmest good choice, and the one that feels most like a neighbourhood rather than a district. Tradeoff: no light rail yet, so budget twenty-five to thirty minutes each way to downtown.

PIONEER SQUARE

The oldest part of the city, rebuilt in red brick and cast iron after the 1889 fire, when the streets were regraded a full storey higher than the original ground floors — which is why there is an underground down there to tour. Cheapest central rooms and a real gallery scene. Tradeoff: quiet and edgy after dark, and stadium crowds on game nights.

QUEEN ANNE

The residential hill north of downtown, walkable down to Seattle Center and home to the viewpoint at Kerry Park that every photo of this city is taken from. Fits couples and anyone travelling with kids who wants calm streets. Tradeoff: almost no nightlife, and the climb back up is not a stroll.

WHEN TO GO — AND WHEN NOT TO

November through March is the cheap window and it is cheap for an honest reason. Seattle sits at 47.6 degrees north — further north than Montreal — so December sunsets land before 4:30pm and the light stays flat all day. The upside is empty museums, hotel rates at their floor, and a city that is still fully functional in the wet. July through early September is the reward season: reliably dry, warm, the mountains out, and priced like everyone knows it.

Watch the calendar. Seattle is a main homeport for Alaska cruises from May to September, and turnaround days fill downtown hotels with thousands of people arriving and departing at once. Seafair in late July and early August brings hydroplane racing and the Blue Angels over Lake Washington. Labor Day weekend carries a large festival at Seattle Center. And in some recent years late August and September have brought wildfire smoke from the interior, which can turn a clear week hazy with no warning.

ONE THING TO SKIP

SKIP THE SPACE NEEDLE TICKET. You are paying a premium to stand in the one spot in Seattle where the view does not contain the Space Needle. That is the whole problem with it. Kerry Park, a short climb up Queen Anne, lines up the Needle, the downtown towers, Elliott Bay and Mount Rainier in a single frame for nothing at all. If you want height with a drink in your hand, the older observation deck downtown or a high hotel bar does the job. If a CityPASS already includes the Needle, fine, ride it. Otherwise put the money on a walk-on ferry to Bainbridge, where fares are collected in one direction only and the skyline sits behind you the whole way.
