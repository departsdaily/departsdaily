THE BRIEFING — TAMPA STRAIGHT UP

Tampa is not a beach town, and the visitors who arrive expecting sand outside the hotel spend their first evening confused and slightly annoyed. It is a working port city on a bay — container cranes, a hockey crowd, universities and hospitals — and the famous white sand is on the far side of the water, thirty to fifty minutes away over a causeway. Book it understanding that and it is one of the better value city trips in Florida. Book it thinking it is Clearwater with an airport attached and you have made a planning error you cannot undo on arrival.

Ybor City is the reason to come. A Spanish cigar manufacturer moved his factories up from Key West in 1885 and built worker housing around them, pulling Cuban, Spanish, Italian and Sicilian families into the same few brick blocks — which is how a Cuban sandwich here ended up with Genoa salami in it, and why the mutual-aid social clubs those communities built are still standing on the same streets. It is a National Historic Landmark District. It also has feral chickens strolling the sidewalks, protected by city ordinance, descended from birds those same families kept in their yards.

It suits travellers who want a city and a beach in one trip and do not mind driving between them, and it suits anyone who would rather watch a ballgame and eat well than queue for a theme park. It does not suit anyone determined to go carless — the good neighbourhoods are cut apart by interstates — and it does not suit anyone who cannot cope with August, which here is genuinely heavy.

WHERE YOU'D ACTUALLY BE STANDING

HYDE PARK AND SOHO

South of downtown across the river, oak-shaded streets of early-1900s bungalows with Bayshore Boulevard running along the water — billed as the longest continuous sidewalk in the world at about four and a half miles. Walkable dining at Hyde Park Village. Fits couples and runners. Tradeoff: it is quiet, and Ybor nightlife is a rideshare away.

SEMINOLE HEIGHTS

A few miles north along the river, this is where Tampa's independent kitchens and breweries actually live, in converted bungalows and old filling stations. Come here for dinner and you will eat better for less than anywhere downtown. Tradeoff: it is residential and thin on hotels — treat it as somewhere you go, not somewhere you sleep.

ST. PETERSBURG

Across the bay by bridge, roughly half an hour without traffic. A compact, genuinely walkable downtown on the waterfront with the Dali museum, a mural district and a pier. Fits people who would rather have a small city than a big one. Tradeoff: you will be commuting back over the water for anything specifically Tampa.

CLEARWATER BEACH

The powder sand everybody flies in for, forty-plus minutes from the airport at the end of a causeway. Resort strip, sunsets, easy days with kids. Tradeoff: causeway traffic on weekends is punishing, and staying here effectively removes Ybor, downtown and the food scene from your trip.

WHEN TO GO — AND WHEN NOT TO

June through September is when fares and hotel rates bottom out, and the reason is the weather. The heat and humidity are serious, and the Tampa Bay area records more lightning than anywhere else in the continental United States — expect a thunderstorm most afternoons, usually a violent forty minutes that clears by evening. Hurricane season runs June 1 to November 30 and peaks August through October, so book flexible in that stretch. February through April is the sweet spot on weather: dry, mild, and not yet hot.

The spikes are predictable. January through March is snowbird season and rates climb across the whole region. Late January brings Gasparilla, when a pirate ship invades the waterfront, streets close and every downtown hotel sells out. March stacks spring break on top of spring training baseball, which fills hotels across the bay area, not just Tampa. If you want the shoulder without the swelter, aim at late April, early May, or the first half of December.

ONE THING TO SKIP

DON'T BOLT ON ORLANDO. Every year people book a cheap TPA fare and decide to add two Disney days since it is only an hour and a half up the road. It is not. It is roughly eighty-five miles on Interstate 4, a corridor with a long-standing reputation as one of the worst in the country, and in practice you are looking at two hours or more each way at the times you would actually travel. Add park queues and you have spent four days of a five-day trip in a car and a line, and seen neither city properly. If you want a theme park day, Busch Gardens is inside Tampa. If you want Orlando, fly to Orlando.
