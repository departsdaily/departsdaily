THE BRIEFING — NEW ORLEANS STRAIGHT UP

Bourbon Street is one street in a neighbourhood of roughly seventy blocks, and writing off the French Quarter because of it is the most expensive mistake a first-timer makes. The Quarter at eight in the morning — shutters closed, courtyards dripping, someone hosing down a sidewalk, chicory coffee smell two blocks ahead — has almost nothing to do with the Quarter at eleven at night. Same buildings. Different city. Go early at least once and you will understand what people are actually talking about.

The city sits inside a bend of the Mississippi, which is why nobody here navigates by compass. Directions are lakeside and riverside, upriver and downriver, because the street grid twists to follow the water and north genuinely stops helping. It suits walkers, eaters, and anyone comfortable spending an afternoon with no plan at all. It does not suit people who need a schedule, air conditioning outdoors, or a beach — the Gulf is a couple of hours away and the lake is not a swimming destination.

Two things that are true here and almost nowhere else in America. Mardi Gras is a legal state holiday in Louisiana, and Carnival season opens on January 6 — so the parades roll for weeks before Fat Tuesday, and "Mardi Gras prices" describe most of a month, not one day. And you can legally carry an open drink on the street in the Quarter, provided it is in a plastic cup, which is why bartenders hand you a go-cup at the door instead of asking you to finish it. Both facts shape the trip more than any attraction does.

WHERE YOU'D ACTUALLY BE STANDING

MARIGNY

Immediately downriver of Esplanade Avenue: creole cottages and shotgun doubles painted every colour there is, ten to fifteen minutes on foot from the Quarter. Fits people who want music at night and silence at three in the morning. The tradeoff is that blocks change character fast — look closely at where the guesthouse actually sits before you book it.

BYWATER

Further downriver again, low and quiet, with Crescent Park's levee walkway and its arched steel footbridge over the rail line giving you the Mississippi without the crowd. Fits second and third visits. The tradeoff is distance — after dark you are relying on rideshares, and the walk back from the Quarter is longer than it looks on a map.

GARDEN DISTRICT / UPTOWN

Mansions, live oaks and the St. Charles streetcar at the door, with Magazine Street's shops and small restaurants running parallel for miles. Fits couples and light sleepers. The tradeoff is a twenty to thirty minute streetcar ride each way, and the streetcar is not in a hurry.

TREME

Just lakeside of the Quarter across Rampart Street — one of the oldest African American neighbourhoods in the country and the ground the brass band tradition grew out of. Fits people who want that history within walking distance and lower nightly rates. The tradeoff is thin dining options and dim streets; take a car at night.

WHEN TO GO — AND WHEN NOT TO

July and August are the cheapest weeks of the year and they earn it — daily heat indices in the triple digits, thunderstorms most afternoons, and hotel rates that fall by half. If you can handle the heat, the city is yours. The exception is the Essence Festival over the Fourth of July weekend, which fills the downtown hotels solid. The other quiet windows are the back half of January, after the bowl-game crowd leaves and before Carnival builds, and the first two weeks of December.

Carnival is the price event. Fat Tuesday moves with Easter and lands in February or early March, and the two weekends before it cost as much as the day itself. French Quarter Fest in April and Jazz Fest across the final weekend of April into early May both sell the city out months ahead — check the dates before you book anything in spring. October and November are the mildest, driest weeks of the year and the reason locals stop complaining. Hurricane season runs June through November.

ONE THING TO SKIP

SKIP THE UNGUIDED CEMETERY VISIT. St. Louis Cemetery No. 1 on Basin Street — the famous one, the oldest one, the one on every list — has been closed to unaccompanied visitors since 2015 after years of vandalism. Only licensed guides and family members get through the gate, and people walk up to it daily and get turned around. Either book a licensed tour, which is worth it because the tombs need explaining, or spend the time at one of the cemeteries out on Esplanade Avenue, which you can walk into freely. The above-ground tombs are not decoration, incidentally: the water table sits close enough to the surface that burying anything deep never worked.
