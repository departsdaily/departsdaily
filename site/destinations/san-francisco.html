THE BRIEFING — SAN FRANCISCO STRAIGHT UP

San Francisco gets judged by its worst six blocks and it deserves better than that. The city is seven miles square, the rough stretch that dominates the videos is roughly one neighborhood of it, and almost nothing else looks remotely like that footage. It is real and it is visible and you should know where you are walking at night — and it is also a fraction of a city that is, block for block, as beautiful as any in the country. Both things are true at once.

What defines daily life here is weather that changes by neighborhood. Fog rolls in off the Pacific and stalls against the hills, so the western avenues can sit gray and cold at fifty-five degrees while the Mission, sheltered behind Twin Peaks, is in full sun ten degrees warmer — a fifteen-minute bus ride apart. Pack for both, every day. The other thing you will find nowhere else: the cable cars are the last manually operated cable car system on earth, a moving National Historic Landmark, where the operator clamps a lever onto a steel cable running continuously beneath the street.

It suits walkers with good shoes, eaters, and anyone who wants ocean, old-growth redwoods and wine country all within a couple of hours of a small city. It does not suit anyone packing for California summer — you will be cold in July, which is the single most common visitor mistake here. It does not suit tight hotel budgets either; even in the cheap months, rooms cost more than the flight often does. Come for a long weekend, walk the hills, and do not try to drive any of it.

WHERE YOU'D ACTUALLY BE STANDING

THE MISSION

The sunniest neighborhood in the city, protected from the fog by the hills, with taquerias, painted alleyways, and a park slope where the whole city sits out on a clear afternoon. Two BART stations make it easy. The tradeoff: it is uneven after dark, and hotel stock is thin — this is rental territory.

NORTH BEACH

Italian cafés at the foot of Telegraph Hill, an independent bookstore that has been an institution since the 1950s, and easy walking into Chinatown and down to the water. Suits a first visit. The tradeoff: parking is hopeless, Columbus Avenue is loud on weekends, and every direction out is uphill.

HAYES VALLEY

Flat, low-key and central, between the opera house and the Lower Haight, with small shops, patios and good transit in every direction. Right if hills are a genuine concern. The tradeoff: it is small, has no marquee sight of its own, and ends abruptly at the edges.

THE RICHMOND

The avenues stretching west along the north side of Golden Gate Park toward Ocean Beach — the city's deepest Chinese and Russian cooking and the closest thing to residential San Francisco. The tradeoff: the foggiest, coldest corner of the city, and a long ride back downtown.

WHEN TO GO — AND WHEN NOT TO

November through March is the deals window and it is also the wet season, with December through February the rainiest. Rain here tends to arrive in bouts rather than settling in for days, and room rates fall far enough to make the gamble sensible. June through August is not summer in any sense a visitor expects: fog, wind, and gray mornings that may never burn off at all on the western half of the city. September and October are the real warm season — clear, still, and priced accordingly.

Conference weeks are what actually break the budget. A single large healthcare investor conference downtown in January turns one week of the cheapest month into a peak, and fall conference season at the convention center can double rates with no warning. Add the big August music festival in Golden Gate Park, Pride weekend at the end of June, and holiday weeks. Check the convention calendar before you pick dates — it moves prices more than the season does.

ONE THING TO SKIP

SKIP DRIVING LOMBARD STREET. The crooked block on Russian Hill has a queue of idling cars waiting a turn at eight hairpins you will take at walking speed while staring at the bumper ahead. You see nothing, you photograph nothing, and you have annoyed people who live there. The picture everyone actually wants is taken from the bottom of the block on foot, and the stairways alongside are free. If you want a genuinely good San Francisco staircase, walk the Filbert Steps down Telegraph Hill instead — wooden stairs through private gardens, with the resident flock of wild parrots overhead. No queue, no car, better views.
