THE BRIEFING — NASHVILLE STRAIGHT UP

Nashville is a company town, and the company is songwriting. That single fact explains more about the place than any list of bars: the people you pass in line at coffee are pitching, co-writing, cutting demos or playing on somebody else's record, and the whole social economy runs on who is in the room when a song gets written. Understand that and the city stops looking like a party and starts looking like an industry that happens to be audible from the sidewalk.

The industry is physically clustered in a way that no other American city can show you. Music Row is a few blocks of 16th and 17th Avenues South where the publishers and studios operate out of converted brick bungalows with porches — an entire genre run out of houses. Downtown, the Ryman opened in 1892 as a tabernacle built by a riverboat captain who got religion, which is why you watch shows from wooden church pews. And in Centennial Park there is a full-scale concrete Parthenon with a gilded, forty-two-foot Athena inside, put up for an 1897 exposition and kept because the city liked it.

It suits anyone who will go out on a Tuesday and actually listen — the rooms that matter here are the quiet ones, where four writers pass a guitar and explain the songs between them. It suits food people too; the city eats well beyond the one dish it is known for. It does not suit anyone expecting a compact, old, genteel Southern city — downtown is cranes and new glass, and the good neighborhoods are spread across the river and down the hill. And if crowds in matching shirts wear on you, do not come Friday to Sunday.

WHERE YOU'D ACTUALLY BE STANDING

EAST NASHVILLE

Across the Cumberland River, all porches, bungalows and small stages, centered loosely on the Five Points intersection. This is where the players who back the touring acts actually live and drink, and the music is better and cheaper than downtown. The tradeoff is that you are rideshare-dependent and a bridge away from Broadway.

12 SOUTH

A dozen walkable blocks of shops, coffee and patios south of downtown with a proper park at the end of the strip. Pleasant, safe and easy in the morning. The tradeoff is scale — it is small and residential, the photo lines on the sidewalk are real, and you will leave the neighborhood for a night out.

GERMANTOWN

Nineteenth-century brick streets just north of downtown, with the farmers' market and the ballpark on its edge and restaurant rooms that fill up on weeknights. Walkable to the capitol and calm after dark. The tradeoff is that it is compact and prices like a neighborhood that has already been discovered.

MIDTOWN & MUSIC ROW

The wedge between the Vanderbilt campus and the Row, which puts you within walking distance of the studios, the Parthenon and a wall of student bars. Good value midweek. The tradeoff is football Saturdays, when it is loud and full, and a general shortage of charm.

WHEN TO GO — AND WHEN NOT TO

January and February are the value window and August is the second one, for opposite reasons: winter is grey and mild with the occasional ice event that shuts the city down entirely, and August is heat and humidity in the nineties with a thunderstorm most afternoons. March through May brings the prettiest weather and Middle Tennessee's severe-weather season with it — beautiful days interrupted by genuine tornado watches. October is what everyone wants and the rates say so.

Beyond the June festival week that empties the hotel inventory, watch for the country awards show in November, the big festival an hour southeast in mid-June that moves airport traffic in both directions, Titans home Sundays through the fall, the bowl game right after Christmas, and the Fourth of July fireworks downtown. Weekends are their own event year-round here — bachelorette parties book Friday and Saturday solid. Fly Tuesday or Wednesday and the same trip costs noticeably less.

ONE THING TO SKIP

SKIP THE HOTTEST HOT CHICKEN. Every hot chicken counter in town has a top tier with a joke name, and ordering it is a dare, not a meal. The heat comes from a cayenne paste laid on after the fry — kitchens handle it with gloves, and it is served on white bread with pickles because you will need both. At the top setting the capsaicin stops being flavor and becomes an event, and people genuinely spend the evening in the bathroom instead of at the show they bought tickets for. Order medium. It is already hotter than anything you have at home, and you will still taste the chicken.
