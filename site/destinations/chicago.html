THE BRIEFING — CHICAGO STRAIGHT UP

Chicago is the cheapest big American city to visit well, and the reason is not a flattering one: fewer people live here now than did in 1950. The city was built for a population it no longer has, which means wide streets, deep housing stock, room to breathe, and commercial rents that still let a good restaurant open somewhere other than downtown. That surplus is the whole traveler advantage. You get big-city scale and infrastructure without the crush or the pricing that usually comes attached.

Two facts tell you most of what you need to know about the place. First, in 1900 engineers solved a drinking water crisis by reversing the Chicago River so it drains away from Lake Michigan instead of into it — the city literally turned a river around and it has run backwards ever since. Second, the street grid is numbered from the intersection of State and Madison, and eight hundred address units equal one mile in every direction. Once you know that, any address in the city tells you exactly how far out you are standing. No other American city hands you a map that clean.

It suits people who will get on the L and go somewhere, eat in a neighborhood, and come back. It suits architecture nerds enormously. It does not suit anyone whose plan is to stay inside a six-block downtown radius for four days — the Loop empties out after working hours, and the actual city is north, west and south of it. And the winter is not a punchline. Wind coming off the lake in January is a physical event.

WHERE YOU'D ACTUALLY BE STANDING

WEST LOOP

Old meatpacking and warehouse blocks just across the river from downtown, now the densest restaurant district in the city along Randolph Street. Green and Pink Line trains put the Loop three minutes away. It fits people whose trip is organized around dinner. The tradeoff is cost and reservations — this is the hardest table market in Chicago.

WICKER PARK AND LOGAN SQUARE

Northwest along the Blue Line, which means you are sitting on the O'Hare train. Six-way intersections, old two-flats, record shops and bars that stay open genuinely late. It fits repeat visitors and night people. The tradeoff is distance from the museums and the lakefront, roughly twenty-five minutes each way.

LINCOLN PARK

North of downtown between the brownstone blocks and the lake, with a large free zoo, the beach path and a conservatory inside walking distance. It fits families and early risers. The tradeoff is that it is sedate after dark and the hotel selection is thin — most stays here are rentals.

PILSEN

Southwest of downtown, historically Czech and then Mexican, with murals across whole building faces and a free museum of Mexican art. It fits travelers who want the texture of a working neighborhood. The tradeoff is limited lodging and a longer Pink Line ride home at night.

WHEN TO GO — AND WHEN NOT TO

January through March is when the rates collapse, and they collapse because the weather here in deep winter is a genuine adversary rather than an inconvenience. Wind off the lake through the downtown canyons is the part people underestimate. If you can dress for it, February in Chicago is a serious bargain — the museums and the restaurants are the same buildings they are in July, just emptier.

Summer is festival season and priced for it, with the big music weekend in Grant Park at the turn of July into August, the lakefront air show in August, and the marathon in October. There is also a wildcard no other city has to the same degree: the convention center on the south lakefront is the largest in North America, and one big trade show can spike downtown midweek rates in any month with no relationship to the season. Check dates before booking. September and early October are the sweet spot — warm water, mild air, summer pricing gone.

ONE THING TO SKIP

SKIP NAVY PIER. It is a carnival bolted onto a pier, and it charges accordingly. The rides cost money, the food is worse and dearer than anything you would eat three blocks inland, and the crowd in summer is thick enough to slow you to a shuffle. The only real argument for it is the skyline view, and that argument fails, because the free skyline view worth having is from the lakefront point out past the planetarium on the Museum Campus, where the whole downtown wall lines up across the water at no charge. Do that at sunset instead and put the pier money toward dinner in the West Loop.
